Signal → Symbol

Text, pictures, sound, video — the model doesn't read any of them directly. It only reads numbers.

Whatever you send in — a sentence, a photo, a voice clip — gets turned into the same kind of thing before the model ever looks at it: a long list of numbers. Below is how that happens for each type of content.

Wait — don't images and audio get sent as "base64"?

Yes — and it's worth seeing step by step, because it's easy to mix up with the "understanding" part. Walk through it below ↓

Interlude · Packaging, not understanding

How a file becomes safe to put inside a text message

Picture a mail system that only accepts flat postcards — nothing fragile, nothing oddly shaped. If you need to send a glass sculpture through it, you'd melt it down into flat, standard letters, mail those, then rebuild the sculpture at the other end. Base64 does the same job for a photo or a voice clip: it rewrites the raw data using only 64 plain text characters (letters, digits, and two symbols), so it can safely ride inside a text-only message like a JSON request. Let's walk through it with a real example — the text "Hi!".

Step 1 of 4

Start with the raw bytes

Every file — text, a photo, a sound clip — is really just a row of numbers, each between 0 and 255. Here are the 3 numbers behind the text "Hi!".

Why 0–255?

Each of these numbers is stored in a byte — 8 bits. 8 bits can only be arranged in 256 different ways, so counting from 0, the highest one is 255. It's not a special range someone picked; it's just every combination 8 bits can make.

Step 2 of 4

Write each number in binary

Computers store each number as 8 bits (1s and 0s). Three numbers → 24 bits in a row. Each color below is one original byte.

Why binary? Why exactly 8 of them?

Binary comes from the hardware, not the math: circuits are built from switches, and a switch with just two states — on or off — is the simplest thing to build cheap and reliable at huge scale. 1s and 0s are just those two states, counted. Nothing forces the grouping to be 8 — early computers tried other sizes — but "8 bits" (a byte) became the standard because it was enough to fit every English letter, digit, and symbol with room to spare.

Step 3 of 4

Cut the same 24 bits differently: into groups of 6

This is the actual trick behind base64. Nothing is added or removed — the exact same 24 bits are just re-sliced into 4 groups of 6 instead of 3 groups of 8.

Why groups of 6, and why 3 bytes?

Base64's alphabet has 64 safe characters, and 6 bits is exactly enough to count from 0 to 63 (2⁶ = 64) — one 6-bit chunk, one character. And 24 (the bit total for 3 bytes) is the smallest number that both 8 and 6 divide into evenly. So every 3 bytes converts to exactly 4 characters with nothing left dangling — that's the whole reason base64 works 3 bytes at a time.

Step 4 of 4

Turn each 6-bit number into a text character

A 6-bit number can only be 0–63 — which is exactly why base64 uses an alphabet of 64 safe characters. Each group of 6 bits becomes one letter.

Result: "Hi!" becomes "SGkh" — four plain characters, safe to drop into any text message.

The part that matters most: this whole process is just repackaging. It's fully reversible, and it carries no meaning — the model never looks at "SGkh" and tries to interpret it. The moment it arrives, it gets decoded straight back into the original bytes. Only then does the real understanding step begin: the tiling, the spectrogram, the tokens — everything in the tabs below.

The whole idea in one sentence: the file leaves as bytes, disguises itself as text so it can travel, travels, takes the disguise off when it arrives — and only then does the process of actually "understanding" it begin.

Step 1 · Text

Words get cut into small pieces

A program called a tokenizer chops the sentence into small chunks — sometimes a whole word, sometimes just a piece of one. Common words often stay whole; rare ones get split up. Each chunk is given a number, and that number points to a list of numbers the model already learned during training — its starting "meaning" for that chunk.

A photograph of a small dog running.

This is just a big lookup table: chunk number 4187 always points to the same starting list of numbers. What that list actually "means" isn't written down anywhere — the model works it out from seeing that chunk used millions of times during training.

Step 2 · Image

A picture gets cut into small squares

The image is sliced into small square tiles, a bit like a puzzle — often around 14 or 16 pixels per side. Each tile is flattened out into a single row of numbers. A "position tag" is added to each one too, so the model still knows where in the picture that tile came from, even after it's just a list of numbers.

Shown here as a simple 6×4 grid so it's easy to follow — a real photo is usually cut into hundreds of tiles, then trimmed down before it reaches the model.

A single photo can easily produce over 500 tiles — that's a lot. So most models squeeze that number down first, and only a smaller set (maybe a few dozen to a couple hundred) actually reaches the part of the model that reads your text, so one image doesn't completely bury the conversation.

Step 3 · Audio

Sound turns into a picture, then gets sliced too

The sound is cut into tiny time slices, each about 25 thousandths of a second long. Each slice gets turned into a little chart showing which tones — low or high — are present in it. Line up all these charts side by side and you get something that looks like an image, called a spectrogram. From there, it's handled almost the same way a picture is: cut into pieces and turned into numbers.

Interesting detail: sound and pictures end up going through a very similar step. Both get turned into a grid — rows and columns of pixels, or rows and columns of tones over time — before that grid gets cut into pieces.

Step 4 · Video

A video is just lots of pictures, with a "too much data" problem

A video is treated as a stack of separate pictures, taken one after another. Using every single one would be far too much — so the model only picks out some frames, maybe one per second instead of the 24-30 a video actually has. Each frame it keeps gets cut into tiles just like a normal picture. Even then, that's still a lot of tiles, so one more squeezing step merges nearby frames together to save room.

This is why a model can usually describe one picture in a lot of detail, but often struggles to follow fast movement in a video — a lot of what happened between the frames it kept gets thrown away before it ever reaches the model.

Then it all becomes one thing

Everything ends up the same size and shape

One last small step lines everything up: whatever list of numbers came out — from a word, a tile of an image, or a slice of sound — gets resized to exactly the same length as the others. During training, the model gradually learns to place numbers that "mean" similar things close together in this space, no matter which type of content they came from.

What the model actually reads

This is the real thing it sees: one long line of same-shaped numbers, all mixed together. It doesn't have a separate "looking at a picture" mode — it treats every position in that line the same way, whether it came from a tile of a photo or a word. A few special marker tokens (like <image_start>) are usually dropped in so the model can learn where one type of content ends and another begins.

Reality check

Where exactly does base64 fit into all this?

Right at the very start, before any of the steps on this page. When you send a picture or a voice clip, it usually gets wrapped in base64 first — a way of writing raw file bytes using only plain text characters, so the file can safely travel inside a text-based request. The system unwraps that base64 back into the original file the moment it arrives. Everything shown on this page — cutting into tiles, turning into a spectrogram, and so on — happens after that unwrapping. Base64 is packaging for the trip over the internet, not something the model reads or understands.

Does the model really "see" or "hear", the way a person does?

No. By the time anything reaches the model, it's already been turned into numbers chosen to be useful for prediction — not into a picture or sound the model experiences. There's no image sitting "behind" the numbers that the model looks at. The numbers are all it has.

Is this exactly how Claude works?

This page describes the general approach used across many published multimodal models — cutting images into tiles, turning sound into a spectrogram, lining everything up in the same space. Anthropic hasn't published the exact details of Claude's own encoders, so treat this as "how this type of model usually works" rather than a confirmed diagram of Claude specifically.

Why do models sometimes struggle to count objects in a picture, or follow fast motion in a video?

Both come from information being squeezed earlier in the pipeline. A tile of an image summarizes a small area rather than keeping every pixel exact, and skipping video frames — plus merging several together — can throw away exactly the detail a question needs, like an exact count, or a quick movement that happened between two frames that got skipped.
