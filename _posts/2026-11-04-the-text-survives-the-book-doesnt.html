A reflection

What gets lost when we decide that the data is all that matters?

7 min read

Imagine buying a book, opening it for the last time, and cutting off its spine.

The pages are fed into an industrial scanner, turned into a digital file, and the paper is discarded. The words survive. The book doesn't. In a matter of seconds, an object that survived decades on a library shelf ceases to exist.

Now imagine doing this millions of times.

Each spine is one copy, with its own history.

IThe project

"Project Panama"

This is essentially what Anthropic did through an internal project known as Project Panama. In January 2026, court documents revealed that the company had spent tens of millions of dollars buying physical books, cutting off their bindings and scanning their pages to build a digital library for AI training.

An internal document described the ambition as an effort to:

Internal documentAnthropic

"destructively scan all the books in the world."

UNSEALED

The phrase is striking.

I work in AI, so I understand why data matters. Large language models need enormous amounts of text to learn how language works, how ideas connect and how arguments develop. Books are particularly valuable because they offer depth and context that shorter online content often lacks.

But my concern is not why an AI company needs so many books. It is what we might lose when we focus only on the raw data they contain.

IIThe method

When efficiency becomes the priority

Why dismantle a book when nowadays industrial scanners can digitise it intact?

The answer is practical: removing the binding allows pages to pass through high-speed scanners in batches. It saves time and money.

Scan it whole
Book opened on a cradle→ Pages turned one by one→ Book goes back on the shelf
Slower.
The object survives.
Scan it destructively
Spine cut off→ Loose pages fed in batches→ Paper discarded
Faster, cheaper.
The object is gone.

From a purely operational perspective, destructive scanning is a logical engineering choice. But is efficiency enough reason to destroy something when another option exists?

IIIThe shift

The Shift from Illegal Piracy to Legal Destruction (And Why It Remained Secret)

Unsealed Project Panama records reveal a deliberate shift in how AI training data was acquired.

Early in its development, Anthropic downloaded millions of digital books from “pirate online libraries.” Other AI companies, including OpenAI and Meta, faced similar accusations and a growing number of lawsuits from authors.

Anthropic then began buying second-hand books, removing their bindings, scanning their pages to train AI models, and discarding the paper. In June 2025, a US federal judge ruled that this process constituted fair use under US copyright law, but not the piracy, which led to a $1.5 billion legal settlement.

Yet, internal unsealed communications showed a mandate for secrecy:

Internal communicationAnthropic

"We don't want it to be known that we are working on this."

UNSEALED

This raises a question: the practice was legal, but was it ethical? Perhaps they recognised that gap and chose to keep it secret?

The thing is GenAI is evolving faster than legal frameworks can adapt, leaving them unable to address every aspect of these new technological capabilities.

IVThe object

A book is more than its words

Think about an old book you might find in a secondhand shop.

Inside, you may find a long-closed library’s stamp, a dedication to a loved one or the date the book was given as a gift. Turn a few pages and you may discover pencilled disagreements with the author in the margins, a folded corner or even a faint coffee stain.

These details might seem insignificant, but they tell stories that the printed words cannot. Individual physical books accumulate unique historical "fingerprints" that distinguish one physical copy from every other.

Anthropic has stated that none of their data acquisition programs buy and destroy 'rare' or 'antiquarian' books, so we should not assume that irreplaceable books were systematically destroyed.

Still, physical documents can contain information that a digital transcription cannot preserve. This is not just a question of sentiment. Historians, archivists and researchers examine physical documents for precisely these kinds of traces. The object can tell a story that its text does not.

Of course, not every book can or should be preserved forever. Digitization has enormous benefits, from protecting fragile texts to making knowledge accessible to people around the world.

But when millions of physical objects are processed, who decides which ones are worth keeping, and according to what criteria?

VThe inheritance

The knowledge AI depends on

There is another irony in this story.

AI can summarise books, explain historical events, compare philosophical arguments and help people discover ideas they might never have encountered otherwise.

These are genuinely useful capabilities. But the development of these systems also depends on the work of people who wrote, researched and thought long before modern AI existed.

AI is built on a cultural inheritance it did not create.

There is nothing inherently wrong with building on existing knowledge. Human progress has always depended on the ideas and discoveries of previous generations.

But this relationship also creates responsibilities.

The question is not whether AI should use human knowledge. It is how to move forward while recognising the value of the resources that make progress possible.

VIThe choice

What do we choose to preserve?

I keep coming back to the cutting machine.

It is an ordinary industrial tool performing an ordinary task. There is no need to imagine an intention to erase history. The decision can be explained through economics, logistics and engineering.

And perhaps that is precisely what makes it interesting.

Some consequential decisions are not the result of a grand plan. They emerge from a series of practical choices, each one seemingly reasonable on its own.

A little less time.

A little less expense.

A process that works at a larger scale.

Eventually, those choices determine what remains and what disappears.

Perhaps most of the books in this project were replaceable copies. Perhaps their words existed elsewhere. But even if that is true, the broader question remains.

An AI companymay see a book as training data.
A readermay see a source of ideas.
A historianmay see evidence of another time.
A future researchermay discover something in it that none of us currently recognizes as important.

We cannot preserve everything, and digitization is not inherently a loss. But preservation and access are not the same thing. Neither are information and history.

Preservation≠access Information≠history

When we decide that the information contained in something is all we need, how do we know what else we are choosing to lose?

And who should have a say in that decision?

Because once a book has been reduced to a digital file, we may still have its words.

What we no longer have is the opportunity to discover everything else that book might have told us.
