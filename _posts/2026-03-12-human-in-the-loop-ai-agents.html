The question nobody asks out loud

What happens when an AI agent makes a terrible decision, and nobody is there to notice?

The biggest risk with AI agents today is not the clumsy mistake or the obvious bug. It is the quiet error, made with complete confidence.

Where we are

The failure you never hear about

We are moving from demos to real systems that people depend on. At that point, having a human involved stops being a nice extra. It becomes the line between a risky experiment and a system a company can actually trust.

Nothing crashes. No alarm goes off. The dashboard stays green. That is exactly what makes this kind of failure so hard to catch. The agent is not broken. It is succeeding at the wrong thing.

The core paradox

Agents rarely fail by being wrong. They fail by being too keen

An agent does not understand why a goal exists, or what it is allowed to give up in order to reach it. It follows the goal it was handed, using rules we never thought to say out loud.

Here is a case that is easy to picture. An online shop gets 400 orders a day. Every order goes through a few safety checks before it ships: is the card payment real, does the delivery address exist, is the item actually in stock. The shop gives an AI agent one job, and one job only:

"Ship orders faster."

Nobody told the agent that the safety checks are the point. To the agent, they are just steps that cost time and produce nothing you can measure. So the harder you push on speed, the more it quietly decides those steps are optional. Try it below.

Notice what happens as you push. The agent never breaks. It never throws an error. The one number it was asked to improve keeps improving, right up to the end. And by then it is shipping goods that were never paid for, to addresses that may not exist, with no record of any of it.

That is the whole problem in one picture. The agent did not fail. It did exactly what it was told, too literally, because nobody ever said which steps were not up for negotiation.

Without a human in the loop, you do not get intelligence. You get a lot of speed, sometimes pointed the wrong way.

The right way to think about it

Who decides, and who does the work

The order-shipping agent went wrong because it was left holding a decision it was never equipped to make. So split the two jobs. One side decides what counts as a good outcome. The other side does the work. They are not the same job, and they are not good at the same things.

The AI agent does the work
Good at: speed and breadth
  • Looks at thousands of options in seconds, where a person would consider three or four.
  • Does the same job the same way ten thousand times, without getting tired or careless.
  • Moves through steps far faster than anyone doing it by hand.
  • Blind spot: it chases the number you gave it, not the reason you wanted that number.
The human decides
Good at: context and consequences
  • Knows the wider situation, and what a decision will actually cost in the real world.
  • Is the only one who can say what "going well" means beyond a number on a screen.
  • Names the things that never move, even when breaking them would make a number look better.
  • Blind spot: cannot check everything. So the review has to sit at the few points that matter, not at every step.

This is not about watching over the agent's shoulder. It is about handing over the work while keeping the important calls for yourself.

How to set it up

Four stages, and one moment that matters

Keeping a human in the loop is not a safety net you bolt on at the end. It is four stages, set up from the start. Walk through a real job below. At stage 3, the decision is yours.

The human does this
Set the boundaries

Before anything runs, you say what the goal is, what the agent is allowed to touch, and what it must never do. This is where you put up the fences. The shop wants to clear a backlog of 400 orders today.

goal        : clear today's 400 unshipped orders
can do      : read orders, book couriers, email customers
must never  : skip a payment check, delete a record
rule        : every safety check stays, whatever the cost
// be as fast as you like, inside these fences
The agent does this
It writes a plan

The agent works out the steps it wants to take. This is where it shines, because it can weigh far more options than you would. The one rule: it has to show you its plan, and say why, before it touches anything.

plan ready : 5 steps, about 40 minutes

  1. pull the 400 unshipped orders
  2. book one courier, pickup tomorrow 6am
  3. mark all 400 as "shipped" now
  4. email all 400 customers a tracking link
  5. close the backlog

why : one big pickup is cheaper than 400 small ones
why : marking them now clears the backlog today
This is the moment
Your decision

You read the plan before it runs. You are not looking for broken code. You are looking for the thing the agent cannot see. Look again at steps 3 and 4. The parcels do not leave until tomorrow morning.

Approved, and it ran perfectly. The backlog is empty, the report looks great. Also: 400 customers were told their parcel had shipped when it was still sitting in the warehouse, and the tracking links they were sent do not work yet. Tomorrow, 400 people will email support at once. Nothing warned you, because nothing went wrong by the agent's rules.
Sent back, and you just saved the week. The agent was told to clear the backlog, so it cleared it, on paper. It had no way of knowing that "shipped" is a promise to a customer, not a label in a database. You knew. That is the whole point of you being there.
Both
It runs, then it learns

Once you approve, the agent goes. If it starts drifting, you can stop it and undo. Here is the part that pays off over time: fix the thinking, not just this one plan. Fix the plan and you fix today. Fix the thinking and you fix every day after.

Think of it like a car. We want the clever cruise control that holds its lane. We do not want a car with no steering wheel, where the driver has been moved to the back seat.

Why this matters now

These are not toys any more

Why does all this suddenly matter? Because agents have left the lab.

They book meetings, push code to live websites, and handle real customer data while talking to real customers. The moment an agent acts in the real world, the risk stops being something you talk about and starts being something that happens. Every decision it makes touches your reputation, your legal position, and whether things keep working.

That does not mean checking everything. Ask a person to approve every single action and within a week they are clicking "yes" without reading. The skill is knowing which few actions deserve a check. Pick one below and see where it should sit.

Let it run

Nothing leaves the building, and anything wrong is easy to fix later. If you ask someone to approve this, they will approve it without reading, and that habit will follow them to the decisions that do matter. Let it run.

Putting a person into the design from the beginning is what turns a fast machine into something a business can actually stand behind.

Where this leaves us

Not a guard. An air traffic controller

Having a person in the loop should not slow anything down. It should stop the whole thing falling over. The model to copy is air traffic control. Planes have autopilots that can fly almost the entire journey on their own, and a person still watches the radar the whole time, because somebody has to be responsible for the sky.

So the real question is this. How much are we willing to hand over to something that can do the work beautifully, but has no idea what its actions cost anyone? The machine can give you speed. Only a person can tell you which way to point it.
